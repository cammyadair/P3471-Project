On 7 October 2023, Hamas, the paramilitary group that governs the Gaza Strip, launched an attack on Israel, killing around 1,200 people—primarily Israelis—and taking 240 people hostage. The events that followed have been catastrophic. Israeli military operations in Gaza have caused more than 175,000 injuries and over 74,000 deaths; a further 1,100 people have been killed in the West Bank. These figures include nearly 22,000 children.

On 16 September 2025, following the onslaught in Gaza, the UN's Independent International Commission of Inquiry declared that Israel had committed acts of genocide. The ensuing crisis has prompted mass protests across the UK, with many voicing anger at the government's support for Israel. Notably, media narratives often frame the conflict as beginning on 7 October 2023, a portrayal that obscures its history, which stretches back more than a century. This piece explores that history, discussing Britain's critical involvement in advancing Zionist aspirations and assisting the creation of the state of Israel, all of which led to the displacement of millions of Palestinians and, now, the “first live-streamed genocide in history.”

Before we dive into the history of Britain and the Zionist Project, a quick definition of Zionism helps with understanding. Oxford English Dictionary provides an apt definition:

Zionism
Originally: a movement among Jewish people for the re-establishment of a Jewish nation in Palestine. Later: a movement for the development and protection of the state of Israel. Also: advocacy of or support for this.

Zionism was established as a political movement in 1897 at the initiative of Theodor Herzl (1860–1904). The state of Israel was established in 1948.

The story ahead

This piece traces that history in five stages: Zionism's origins in late nineteenth-century Europe; Britain's wartime promises and the Balfour Declaration; the Mandate years and the Arab Revolt; the withdrawal and partition of 1947–48; and the legacy of that period, from the Nakba to Britain's role today.

Roots: Zionism before Britain (1881 to 1914)

Zionism began not in Palestine or Britain, but in the Russian Empire. After Tsar Alexander II was assassinated in 1881, anti-Jewish riots known as pogroms swept across the region. The following year, Russian Jewish physician Leon Pinsker published “Auto-Emancipation”. He argued that Jews could not be secure as a minority in other people's states and needed a homeland of their own. His supporters formed Hovevei Zion, meaning “Lovers of Zion.”

Between 1882 and 1903, about 25,000 Jews, mostly from Russia and Romania, moved to Ottoman Palestine and established farming colonies. Some estimates are higher, and many later left. They arrived in a land of just under half a million people, overwhelmingly Arab Muslims and Christians. Ottoman authorities tried, with limited success, to restrict Jewish immigration and land purchases.

The movement took on a modern political form under Theodor Herzl, a Vienna-based journalist who had reported on antisemitism during France’s . In 1896, he published Der Judenstaat (“The Jewish State”). A year later, he convened the First Zionist Congress in Basel. The Congress founded the Zionist Organization and adopted the , calling for a Jewish home in Palestine secured under public law. For this reason, 1897 is often seen as the birth of political Zionism.

Palestinian Arabs were already voicing concern. In the decades before 1914, Arab notables and newspapers discussed immigration and land purchases. In 1899, Jerusalem notable Yusuf Diya al-Khalidi warned the chief rabbi of France that the project would meet resistance.

Britain's first formal role came in 1903, when Colonial Secretary Joseph Chamberlain offered land in British East Africa, in what is now Kenya. Known as the “Uganda scheme,” the proposal went before the Sixth Zionist Congress, which voted 295 to 178 to send a commission to investigate. After Herzl's death, the movement rejected the proposal in 1905. The episode showed that Palestine was not yet the only option and that Britain was already prepared to consider Jewish settlement within its empire, a theme that would return in 1917. By 1914, Jews made up somewhere between 7 and 14 per cent of Palestine's population, depending on the source and who was counted. When war broke out, the future of the Ottoman Empire—and Palestine—was about to be decided by others.

Britain at war, three promises (1914 to 1920)

Two months after Britain declared war on the Ottoman Empire in November 1914, the Zionist cabinet minister Herbert Samuel circulated a memo, The Future of Palestine, to his colleagues. Over the next two years, Britain made three commitments about the same land, none of which could be kept.

The first was to the Arabs. Between July 1915 and March 1916, Sir Henry McMahon, Britain's High Commissioner in Egypt, exchanged ten letters with Hussein, the Sharif of Mecca, promising British support for Arab independence in return for a revolt against the Ottomans. Hussein launched that revolt in June 1916. Whether Palestine fell within the promised territory has been disputed ever since.

The second was to France. In May 1916, Britain and France secretly agreed in the Sykes–Picot Agreement to divide the Ottoman Arab lands between them, with Palestine placed under international administration. This contradicted the terms of the McMahon letters.

The third came on 2 November 1917. Foreign Secretary Arthur Balfour wrote to Lord Rothschild, a leader of Britain's Jewish community, that the government viewed with favour “a national home for the Jewish people” in Palestine. The text was finalised on 31 October and published in the press on 9 November. It promised to protect the civil and religious rights of Palestine's other communities, but it did not name them as Arabs, did not mention their political rights, and no one had asked them. Roughly nine in ten people in Palestine were not Jewish at this time.

Historians still debate why the government did it. The War Cabinet believed a declaration would win support among Jews in Russia and America, at a time when Britain needed both countries to stay committed to the war. Strategic interest in the eastern Mediterranean, sympathy among ministers, including Balfour and Prime Minister David Lloyd George, and the lobbying of Zionist leader Chaim Weizmann also played a part. Lord Curzon and Edwin Montagu, the cabinet's only Jewish member, both opposed it.

The promise came before the conquest. Jerusalem surrendered to British troops on 9 December 1917, and General Edmund Allenby formally entered the city on 11 December, on foot. By October 1918, the Ottomans had capitulated. At the 1919 peace conference, the Arab leader Faisal and Weizmann each made their case, but Balfour privately wrote that year that the wishes of Palestine's Arab inhabitants counted for less than Zionism, to which Britain and its allies were committed.

On 24 April 1920, the San Remo Conference assigned the Mandate for Palestine to Britain and confirmed the Balfour Declaration. The League of Nations Council confirmed it on 24 July 1922, and it took effect in September 1923. Britain now held legal authority over Palestine, with obligations pointing in opposing directions. Palestinian protests began almost immediately.

The Mandate and the Arab Revolt (1920 to 1939)

Britain governed Palestine under a Mandate that committed it to two things at once. The Mandate required Britain to create the political, administrative and economic conditions for a Jewish national home, recognised a Jewish Agency to advise the administration, and encouraged close Jewish settlement on the land. Palestinian Arabs, roughly nine in ten of the population when the Mandate began, were not named as a people. They were the “non-Jewish communities” of the Balfour Declaration.

The population shifted quickly. Jews were about 11 per cent of Palestine in the 1922 census and nearly 17 per cent by 1931. By 1936, as persecution in Nazi Germany drove emigration, estimates put the Jewish share at around 28 per cent.

Tension grew with it. Violence broke out in 1920, 1921 and 1929, the last beginning as a dispute over the Western Wall. A British inquiry into the 1929 violence, the Shaw Commission, found that Jewish immigration and land purchases had fueled Arab fears for their political future and economic security.

In April 1936, the tension turned into a revolt. A general strike, called after two Jews were killed near Tulkarm, escalated into an armed uprising that Palestinians call the Great Revolt. Its demands were independence and an end to unlimited Jewish immigration and land sales. In July 1937, the Peel Commission concluded that Arab and Jewish aspirations could not be reconciled and recommended partitioning Palestine. Palestinians rejected the plan, and the fighting intensified.

Britain crushed the revolt with force. The historian Matthew Hughes, who has studied the army’s records, documents collective punishment, house demolitions, detention without trial and the use of Palestinians as hostages. Another scholar, Charles Anderson, shows that troops used Palestinians as human shields on military routes. The Palestinian historian Walid Khalidi estimates that British forces killed about 3,800 Palestinians. About 260 British security personnel also died, and Palestinian fighters killed Jewish civilians. British officers trained Jewish units, such as Orde Wingate’s Special Night Squads, to fight alongside them. Much of the Palestinian leadership was jailed or exiled.

The revolt’s defeat mattered for what came next. Historians argue that it left Palestinian society weaker, less armed and without its leaders a decade before the war of 1948. That is one of the strongest links in the argument that Britain’s rule helped to bring about the displacement that followed.

But Britain’s policy then turned. Facing war in Europe and wanting calm in the Arab world, it issued the White Paper of May 1939. It rejected partition, promised an independent Palestine within ten years, limited Jewish immigration to 75,000 over five years, with further immigration needing Arab consent, and restricted Jewish land purchases. Zionist leaders saw it as a betrayal of the Balfour Declaration. Britain, which had helped build the Jewish national home, was now trying to limit it, just as Europe’s Jews faced catastrophe.

Withdrawal and partition (1939 to 1948)

The Second World War and the Holocaust transformed a colonial dispute into a refugee crisis. Britain largely upheld the White Paper’s immigration limits throughout the war and after it, turning back refugee ships, including the Exodus in 1947, and detaining many survivors in camps on Cyprus. Zionist leaders concluded that Britain was now an obstacle.

From 1944, the Irgun and Lehi underground groups launched a campaign of violence against British rule, and the main Jewish militia, the Haganah, briefly joined them. On 22 July 1946, the Irgun bombed the British administrative headquarters in the King David Hotel in Jerusalem, killing 91 people: 41 Arabs, 28 Britons, 17 Jews and five others. The Irgun said it had issued warnings; British officials disputed this. The attack further turned British public opinion against remaining in Palestine.

A conference of Jewish and Arab leaders in London ended in deadlock in February 1947, and Britain handed the problem to the United Nations. A UN committee recommended partition, and on 29 November 1947, the General Assembly voted 33 to 13, with 10 abstentions, to divide Palestine into a Jewish state, an Arab state and an international zone around Jerusalem. Britain was one of the abstainers. Jews were about a third of the population, yet the plan gave their state roughly 56 per cent of the land. Jewish leaders accepted it. Palestinian leaders and the Arab states rejected it.

Civil war broke out the next day. Britain, still the governing power until May 1948, had said it would not enforce partition against either side. It did little to stop the fighting and withdrew its forces and officials as the Mandate expired. On 14 May 1948, Israel declared independence, and the armies of five Arab states entered Palestine the following day.

Britain’s influence did not end with its withdrawal. Transjordan’s army, the Arab Legion, was financed by Britain and commanded by a British officer, John Glubb, known as Glubb Pasha. The historian Avi Shlaim argues that Britain gave King Abdullah of Transjordan a "green light" to occupy the parts of Palestine that the UN had assigned to an Arab state, in an understanding with the Zionist leadership that came to be called collusion. A later study of the Legion’s records by Graham Jevon supports part of that claim. Another historian, Matthew Hughes, says military necessity explains the Legion’s moves better than politics. The question remains disputed.
